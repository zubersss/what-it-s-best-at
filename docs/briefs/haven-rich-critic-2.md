## The five biggest gaps

### 1. From 90 m to 1 km the wood is a texture, not trees.

From 90 m to 1 km the wood is a texture, not trees. The canopy is an even field of small round blobs of the same size, like moss, sponge or bubble wrap. No crown stands out by its outline, and no crown is shaded as one mass with a lit side and a shade side. At 1 km the speckle only gets finer; it never gathers into larger soft clumps that follow the ridges. The trees in blossom make it worse. They are flat pink discs (from 300 m, 60 degrees down) or torn pink rectangles (at 20 degrees down and from 90 m), lying on top of the canopy like stickers. They are all one size, spread evenly over the whole wood like polka dots, salmon in sun and flat grey-lilac in shade, with no volume and no shadow. Where the wood meets open ground, the edge is a staircase of square pixels with loose dark squares scattered beyond it. Clearings inside the wood are flat grey-sage or grey-beige patches with torn edges and no grass or tone, and they read as holes in the picture rather than glades.

**Pictures.** 3: whole frame, the pink and lilac dots over the lower two-thirds, the grey torn clearings at bottom left and centre. 6: blue-grey speckled canopy, pink rectangles across the lower half, stepped edges of the orange and yellow clearings at top and lower left. 4: even blob grid on the lit slope, ragged dotted edge against the cream ground on the right. 2: flat pink and lilac blossom 'lily pads' in the foreground, speckle over the middle slope. 1: small pink flecks in the valley wood.

**Why.** This is the owner's first demand: 'perfect from a distance, specially when I'm flying high above it'. The wood fills most of every aerial frame, so it carries the whole impression. As it stands it reads as noise and repetition, not as a painted wood of bold shapes. The evenly scattered blossom decals are the clearest sign of repetition, and they turn the blossom the owner asked for into a flaw.

**What it would need.** From 90 to 300 m, each crown is a rounded mass: a warm yellow-green cap toward the Sun, a cool blue-green shade side, and a small coloured shadow falling onto its neighbours, so the wood has a rhythm of bumps like dabs of paint. Crowns vary in size, and dark fir spires stand among the round ones. At 1 km, neighbouring crowns merge into larger soft clumps in two or three tones that follow slopes and ridges. A cherry seen from above is a domed crown: pale warm pink in the light, mauve in the shade, broken into a few clumps with green between them and a shadow beside it. Blossom gathers in drifts (a wood's edge, a stream, one hillside) with plain green between, and a few lone ones stand in meadows. A forest edge is a soft, irregular line of single crowns casting short shadows onto the open ground. A clearing carries the meadow's ochre and green with a light speckle of flowers.

### 2. The view from high above is the weakest in the set.

The view from high above is the weakest in the set. At 3 km two-thirds of the frame is one flat beige-khaki cloud mass. It has no sunlit top, no coloured shade side and no readable edge, so it looks like dirty fog or a smear on the lens rather than cumulus seen from above. Where the land shows through, it is a murky, almost black navy-green with faint straight lines across it, and a small dark hole sits in the cloud at bottom centre. The far cloud deck on the horizon is rows of broken white horizontal dashes, like scan lines. Clouds seen from the ground have the same problems: the distant small clouds are built from stepped dashes, one big cloud has round blue holes punched through it, a grey smoky column hangs under the cumulus behind the fir, and the cloud right beside the low Sun is still evenly front-lit cream, with no dark body and no glowing rim. The big pink mountain that anchors the opening view is also formless from afar: a smooth salmon dome with soft blotches, no rock faces and no shade side, and its ridge is stair-stepped from the air.

**Pictures.** 5: whole frame (beige deck across the bottom and right, the dark land, the hole at bottom centre, the dashes along the horizon). 8: big cloud at top left with two holes through it, dashed clouds low in the sky. 1: grey column under the big cloud behind the fir, dashed clouds at the left; the pink mountain at upper right. 9: the cloud at top left beside the Sun. 2: the mountain's jagged ridge at upper right. 10: the same smooth mountain.

**Why.** The owner wants to fly high above the world and find it beautiful, and this is the one picture taken high above it. Here the world is hidden, and what shows through is dark. In a painted poster the clouds and the mountains on the skyline are among the boldest shapes, each with a light-and-shade pair. Here both are a single flat tone, which breaks the look and the owner's main demand at the same time.

**What it would need.** At 3 km: fewer, separate cumulus heaps with wide gaps, so most of the land shows. Each heap has a bright warm-cream top where the Sun strikes, a lilac or blue-grey shade side and underside, a clean cauliflower outline, and a soft blue-violet shadow on the ground below. The land between grows paler and bluer with distance, not darker: ridges lit on one side, valleys in coloured shade, wood and meadow in distinct tones. The far cloud deck is soft layered bands fading into the haze. Seen from below, a cloud is solid with no holes; with the Sun behind it, it has a darker grey-violet body and a bright gold rim. The mountain is broken into planes: warm salmon-gold faces toward the Sun, dusty violet faces away from it, a crisp ridge crest, gullies and rock bands near the top, with a paler, bluer range behind.

### 3. The trees the owner asked to see refined are never shown, and what does show is crude.

The trees the owner asked to see refined are never shown, and what does show is crude. In the cherry picture the camera is down in the grass. The frame is straw blades and yellow flower heads; the tree is cut off by the top edge. All that shows is the base of a smooth dark grey-purple trunk with no bark, no fork and no branch, and a few pink specks. A near-black teal hole sits between the blades. No petals stream off downwind, and none lie on the grass. On Earth the nearest trunk is a perfectly straight black pole of even width from ground to frame top, with no taper, no root flare, no bark colour, no branches and no crown in frame, and it splits the view in two. At the opening, the nearest broadleaf beside the fir is a dark green lump of rounded blobs with no limbs. Only the fir has a strong, readable silhouette.

**Pictures.** 11: whole frame (trunk and blossom only at top centre, cut by the frame; the dark hole at bottom centre-right; no drifting petals). 12: black pole right of centre, dark trunk at the left edge, pine sprays only in the top corners. 1 and 10: the round broadleaf right of centre beside the fir.

**Why.** Trees are the owner's sharpest and most repeated complaint: 'the trunks and the leaves still don't look anything like the references ... specially the leaves and branches', and 'trees with flower petals that bloom and ride the wind'. The picture meant to prove both proves neither, so the owner cannot judge the work. What is visible (black poles, a bare smooth stump, a lumpy crown) shows the problem is still there. Black trunks and a black hole also break the rule that shade is coloured, never black.

**What it would need.** Frame a blossoming cherry whole from eye height at about 6 m, with the camera above the grass and the grass at knee height in front. The trunk flares at the root, tapers and leans, and forks into two or three limbs that split into smaller branches visibly reaching into the crown. It is warm grey-brown, its shade side violet or red-brown, its lit side picked out with a hint of bark. The crown is many small, finely cut clusters of petals and leaves, with gaps where branches and sky show through, lit as one mass: pale pink on the Sun side, mauve in the shade, a lacy cut edge. A ribbon of small petals peels off downwind, tumbling and catching the light, crossing in front of the camera and thinning with distance, with a soft pink scatter on the grass beneath and downwind. Earth's pines get a tapering, coloured trunk with branch stubs and limbs, shown with their crown. The base of the grass is deep olive or violet-brown, never black.

### 4. Water from the air reads as a rendering fault.

Water from the air reads as a rendering fault. At 1 km the river is an oily, iridescent ribbon of lilac, mint green, yellow and pink, like petrol on a puddle or holographic foil. The same rainbow mottling shows in patches on flat or wet ground at 300 m and 1 km. At 3 km the lake is a flat, fully saturated royal blue with a stair-stepped outline and a thin cyan rim, like a paint-bucket fill. It takes no colour from the sky and haze, has no shallow margin and no darker centre, and it sits outside the warm, muted palette of everything around it.

**Pictures.** 4: the river across the upper third, the rainbow patch at bottom centre, the cream and rainbow ground at the right. 3: yellow-green-violet streaks in the bottom-right corner. 5: the lake on the left, with its stepped edge.

**Why.** The owner asked for 'water that goes down stream and piles where it can't go further like real life lakes and rivers and oceans'. Rivers and lakes are what the eye looks for from altitude, and these read as bugs, which a viewer notices before anything beautiful. Colours outside the palette and a pure primary blue break the rule of few colours, each time of day a pair of them.

**What it would need.** A river is a calm band in two or three flat tones taken from the sky: pale silver-blue where it catches the light, deeper teal in shade and under cloud shadow, a thin bright line of reflected light along one bank, and a darker band where the wood overhangs the edges. Lakes take the sky and haze colour, paler toward the far shore, with a turquoise or sandy shallow margin, a darker centre and a soft, smooth shoreline. They are matched in value to the poster palette. No other colours appear on water or wet ground.

### 5. At eye height the open country is one even carpet that runs to a ruler-straight horizon.

At eye height the open country is one even carpet that runs to a ruler-straight horizon. The grass is the same straw-gold in every biome, combed into even vertical stripes at one height, with little fresh green, no clumps or seed heads and no wind bands, so the steppe reads as a wheat-field texture. Each place has essentially one flower form: a flat disc or pinwheel facing the camera at the top of the grass, often with no stem showing. The flowers are sprinkled evenly from the feet to the horizon rather than gathered in drifts, and their sizes vary mostly with distance. Variety comes mainly from colour: lilac in the meadow with some pink at the left and blue at the right, yellow daisies on long bare stalks at the opening. The pollen shows only against a low Sun, as round glowing dots of a few fixed sizes spread as thickly high in the open sky as over the flowers, and it reads as lens dust. With the Sun elsewhere there are only a few white specks, and the sky around the low Sun has a coarse blocky grain. Nothing is lost in the air layer by layer: the far grass has the same colour and contrast as the near grass, and on the steppe a row of grey boulders sits along the horizon like beads on a string.

**Pictures.** 7: the whole lower half and the boulders on the horizon. 8: the evenly spread lilac pinwheels and the flat horizon. 9: motes up to the top edge of the sky, the grain round the Sun, the small Sun. 1 and 10: yellow daisies on bare stalks, sparse long strands at bottom left. 11: big flat yellow heads lying at odd angles.

**Why.** The owner asked for grass 'a lot denser', flowers 'more diverse and abundant in certain areas and biomes, different flowers of different sizes that release pollen into the air that makes it beautiful'. Density is now there on flat ground, but the other asks are not met: the flowers show even coverage and one form, and the pollen is a camera effect that shows from one direction only. A field running flat to an empty, sharp horizon also has none of the poster's layered depth.

**What it would need.** Flowers in drifts and pools: patches where one kind is so thick it colours the ground, thinning at the edges into plain grass, with each biome having its own mix and dominant colour. Several forms at several heights: tall spikes of small blooms rising above the grass, flat white umbels, big single heads on leaning stems, and low cushions of tiny flowers among the blades. Heads are tilted and nodding, as often side-on as face-on, with stems and a leaf showing. Grass in clumps of different heights, with lush greens and gold tips in the meadow and tawny and olive on the steppe, lighter seed heads catching the light, and soft lighter and darker bands rolling across with the wind. Pollen is thickest just above the drifts and thins with height. It drifts in loose wisps downwind as small soft flecks, visible against shaded ground with the Sun to the side, and glows larger only when backlit, against a smooth sky. The land rolls: a near swell, a middle band of darker grass or a far line of trees, and each band behind paler and bluer until the far field melts into the sky. Boulders are scattered near and far, the far ones pale. When it is in view, the Sun is a large soft disc with a wide warm halo.

## What works

At eye height the opening view (1) is already a painted poster. Ochre meadow, salmon mountain, slate-blue sky and a cool blue-green valley sit in a warm-light and cool-shade pair, and the wood, sea and far coast fall back into paler, bluer air on the left. The dark tiered fir is a crisp silhouette, and the yellow flowers are a fine scatter of accents. Shade is coloured: from the air, cloud shadows lie on the wood as deep teal, not grey. The worst-case view (10) keeps the place, its layering and its mood, only coarser, and its chunkier tufts and brushy clouds suit the painted look. Grass is now thick on flat ground, with no bald patches (7, 8). The meadow's lilac, pink, white and blue mark the start of the flower variety the owner wants. The backlit meadow (9) is the most beautiful frame in the set: glowing gold grass, a soft Sun and warm motes that make the air feel alive. The steppe's faceted boulders have true light and shade planes, firs still read as dark spires from 300 m, and Earth's canyon rim (12) has the most painterly sky of all, with a canyon that recedes in warm layers of haze and pine sprays with a finely cut edge.
